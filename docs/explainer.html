Step 1

What the government is trying to do

A person from a Scheduled Caste family wants to start something small. A tailoring unit. An e-rickshaw. A kirana shop. A dairy with two cows.

A normal bank would charge them somewhere between 11% and 18% a year, and would usually ask for collateral they do not have. So the government built a parallel, cheaper channel: loans at 6.5% to 8%, covering up to 90% of the project cost, for families earning up to ₹5 lakh a year.

Say it simply The government is trying to be a kinder bank for people the normal bank would turn away.
Who runs it: NSFDC — the National Scheduled Castes Finance and Development Corporation. Set up in 1989, sits under the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment. Not a bank. Not for profit.

Income ceiling of ₹5.00 lakh, both rural and urban, effective 07.01.2026. Source: Standing Committee on Social Justice and Empowerment, 25th Report, presented to Lok Sabha 12.08.2026.

NSFDC told Parliament directly: its portfolio earns an average 3.1% a year, so without fresh equity its asset base grows about 3% while inflation runs 4 to 4.5%. In its own words, it "may not grow" and cannot "meet even the inflation."

A judge will ask "So it is shrinking in real terms?" — Yes. And beneficiaries nearly halved in FY25, which the Committee links directly to the equity freeze.

Step 4

Why the middleman exists

It looks wasteful until you see the arithmetic. NSFDC has 70 sanctioned posts and only 4 liaison centres for the entire country. It cannot assess a farmer in Sitapur or recover a loan in Bahraich from Delhi.

The State Channelizing Agency is the state's own SC finance corporation, with district offices and staff who know the ground. That is the theory.

In practice: SCAs are under-performing or non-performing in 14 states. Telangana and Ladakh have never appointed one at all. NSFDC has been quietly routing around them, growing its alternate bank partners from 17 in 2016 to 53 today.
A judge will ask "Did adding banks fix it?" — No. Beneficiaries through the SCA channel fell from 1,18,002 in FY25 to 61,394 in FY26, roughly halving in one year. And those bank partners are excluded from any Credit Guarantee Scheme, so they cannot lend large amounts safely.
Step 5

The gate nobody tells you about

Before a partner can receive fresh money from NSFDC, it must clear published conditions:

Partner typeCondition
SCA / CAGovernment or bank guarantee in place
SCA / CANo overdues to NSFDC older than one year
SCA / CAAt least 80% of previous funds already used
Regional Rural BankNet NPA below 15% in 3 of the last 6 years
Public Sector BankNo overdues at the time of disbursement
This is the heart of the problem statement. A citizen can be perfectly eligible, pick the right scheme, walk into the nearest partner office — and that partner may be blocked from receiving funds. Nobody tells them. There is no public list. They simply wait.
Step 6

What it actually costs

NSFDC lends to the partner at 5%. The partner adds a margin — usually 4%, but up to 10% on microfinance, because, as NSFDC told Parliament, "failure in microfinance is a necessary incidence." The margin is priced for expected defaults.

So the same "NSFDC loan" costs different people very different amounts. Parliament called the 15% rate "very high" and a reason people do not borrow.

Break 4

Nobody upstream can see any of it

NSFDC monitors 91 channel partners through 4 liaison centres. Its board has 9 of 15 seats vacant. Staff vacancies rose from 21 to 27 between March and July 2026 — they are getting worse, not better.

A journalist asked the president of Madhya Pradesh's SC Finance Corporation how his own schemes were performing. His answer:

"I am not aware of the progress of the schemes, I will be able to tell something only after I have statistics."

In the same state: 6,027 people applied for a scheme with 2,000 places. 1,119 were funded. A separate scheme approved 21 projects and funded zero.

The government's own diagnosis

Asked in Parliament in February 2026 why funds go under-utilised, the government listed four reasons:

  1. Delays in beneficiary selection by State Channelizing Agencies
  2. Time taken by beneficiaries to complete loan documentation
  3. Non-receipt of utilisation information from district branches
  4. Pending utilisation certificates
Note what is not on that list: lack of demand, and lack of money. The bottleneck is selection, paperwork, and information never travelling back up.

And the last break feeds the first. Defaults make a partner ineligible. An ineligible partner turns away the next applicant. Less money flows back to NSFDC, which is already getting no fresh equity. The failures compound in a loop, not a line.
